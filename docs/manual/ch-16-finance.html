The Money Room — Purchase Orders, Payroll & Cost Report

@@CH16@@

Chapter 08 describes how a budget is arrived at. This chapter describes what happens to that budget once the production starts spending against it: how a dollar is committed, how it becomes an actual, and how the weekly cost report decides that an account is going to land over.

How this chapter was written. Every statement below comes from reading the files it names — nothing here was observed on a running screen. Cinamate is worked on continuously. Where the page in front of you contradicts a sentence here, believe the page.

What the Money Room is, and what it is not

The Money Room is finance/index.html, driven by finance/lib-money.js (CMoney). It keeps a register of committed and spent money, joins that register to the budget sheet by account number, and produces the weekly cost report a line producer takes to the studio and the completion bond.

It is not a general ledger: no double entry, no journal, no trial balance, no period close. It files nothing with anyone. It does not talk to a bank, a card processor or a payroll bureau — no money moves because you typed it here. And it is not accounting advice; the timecard engine's own docstring in tools/lib-money.js says as much, calling its rules published industry conventions parameterised for your own agreements and explicitly "not payroll or legal advice". Every figure here is a production's working estimate of its own position, to be reconciled against the books your accountant actually keeps.

The one store

Everything the room owns lives in browser storage under SB_Money_v1, in the shape CMoney.blank() returns: { v, pos, petty, etc, labor, snapshots, nextPo }. It is local to the browser you are sitting at — there is no server copy, and a second machine sees a different Money Room. Purchase orders are numbered from nextPo, which starts at 1001, so the first PO a production raises is PO-1001.

Purchase orders

Raise one from the Purchase orders bar: vendor, what for, an account from the dropdown, an amount. The page refuses to issue a PO with a non-positive amount. A new PO is created with status open, and the status select offers four values in a deliberate order:

StatusColumn on the cost reportMeaning
openCommittedRaised, not yet billed. The money is spoken for.
invoicedActualBilled. Treated as real money whether or not it is paid.
paidActualIdentical treatment to invoiced.
voidnoneSkipped entirely — neither committed nor actual.

Petty cash is the other half of the same register: who, what for, an account, an amount. A petty line is always an actual. There is no open state for a receipt, because a receipt is money that has already gone.

The Money Room is not the only place POs are raised. Other modules write directly into SB_Money_v1 as work is awarded, each naming its own account: casting on committing an offer (4000), deal memos on being marked signed, VFX on awarding a shot bid, Post and Music (15000), Locations on a permit fee (13000), Wardrobe (10000), and Safety for paid-duty police (3000) and an animal coordinator (9000). Most stamp the PO number back onto the record that raised it, so a shot or a cue can be matched to its commitment later.

Removing a line is immediate and final. The ✕ button on a PO or a petty row asks once and then drops the row from SB_Money_v1. There is no undo and no history — a deleted commitment is simply gone from the next report.

The chart, and the account that is not in it

Account numbers come from js/lib-money-accounts.js (CAccounts), which is the single chart every module that posts money shares. It holds twenty major accounts — 1000 Story & Rights through 19000 Contingency, plus 20000 Payroll Fringes — and fourteen detail accounts that roll up to them: 4100, 4200, 4400 and 4500 under Cast; 8500 under Grip & Electric; 9100 and 9900 under the Art Department; 13500 under Locations; 15200, 15400, 15600 and 15800 under Post-Production; 16500 and 16800 under Insurance & Legal. A posting on a detail account is shown against its major account's budget line when only the major line is budgeted, so a department can be precise without becoming unbudgeted.

An account the chart does not recognise rolls up to itself. It does not vanish into a neighbouring line and it is not silently dropped: it appears on the cost report as its own row, named Unbudgeted · <acct>, with a budget of zero. Because variance is budget minus estimated final cost, a zero budget makes that row permanently and unfixably over. Cast is 4000. There was a period when the casting office committed offers to 1400, which exists nowhere in the chart, and every cast offer therefore surfaced as a permanently over-budget Unbudgeted · 1400 line that nothing could reconcile. That is the exact shape of the mistake this row exists to expose.

How to tell: scan the Category column for the word Unbudgeted. A row carrying it is either an account you never budgeted, or a typo. Both are worth finding on a Tuesday rather than at the bond's audit.

The account dropdown is seeded from your budget sheet, not from the chart. finance/index.html builds the PO and petty-cash selects from the accounts present in the saved SB_BudgetSheet_v1. With no sheet saved it falls back to a hardcoded list of 1100, 2000, 3000 and 5000 — and 1100 is not an account in the chart. On a brand-new production, build the top sheet first, or the first PO you raise from that empty fallback lands as Unbudgeted · 1100.

Payroll and timecards

Labour is half to two-thirds of a picture, and it reaches the report through js/lib-payroll.js (CPayroll), which joins the timecard log to the cost report and does nothing else. Cards are logged in Tools → Timecards and live under SB_Timecards_v1; the overtime, meal-penalty and turnaround arithmetic is TMoney.timecard's and is not reimplemented here (see Chapter 19).

Labour postings are derived, not stored. They are rebuilt from the cards on every render, so SB_Money_v1 never holds a stale copy of a card you have since corrected — and, conversely, you cannot delete a labour line from the Money Room. Fix the timecard.

One day of one person produces up to two postings:

  • Labour — gross plus penalties, on the department's own account. The account comes from the card if it carries one and that account exists in the chart; otherwise CAccounts.forRole reads the role. A gaffer lands on 8000, not on Direction. Unmatched crew defaults to 5000, unmatched cast to 4000.
  • Fringes — on 20000, never on the department. Fringes are cross-departmental; posting them into the department would overstate every department and hide the single number a completion bond asks for first.

A card's status decides the column: scheduled, committed or projected post as committed — the crew is hired and the day is on the board — anything else posts as actual, and void posts nothing. Older rows that carry only the "gross+fringe total" column are still postable: the total is split back into its halves with fringes as the residual, so the two postings add back to the logged figure exactly. A card with no rate, call and wrap, no gross and no total is reported as an error under the payroll panel rather than posted as a silent zero, and a bad call or wrap time behaves the same way.

Hot costs

Tools → Hot Costs is a daily actual-versus-budget view with its own posting register, keyed SB_HotCost_v1, reported by TMoney.hotCost. It groups by account, splits actual from committed (a posting is actual or po), seeds its budget column from the saved top sheet, and marks an account HOT above 85% used and OVER past 100%.

Hot costs and the Money Room are two separate sets of books. Nothing joins SB_HotCost_v1 to SB_Money_v1. A PO raised in the Money Room does not appear in Hot Costs, and a hot-cost posting does not appear in the cost report. The daily production report reads the hot-cost store only. Pick one as your register of record and use the other as a working view, or the two will disagree.

The cost report

Six columns, per account, plus a TOTAL row:

ColumnWhat it is
BudgetThe budget sheet's estimate for that account, read through js/lib-money-sheet.js so an Amt × Units × Rate line counts for what the calculator says it is worth.
ActualInvoiced and paid POs, all petty cash, and worked timecards.
CommittedOpen POs, signed deals and scheduled crew days.
ETCEstimate to complete. Defaults to whatever is left in the plan — budget minus actual minus committed, floored at zero — and is editable per line.
EFCActual + Committed + ETC.
VarianceBudget − EFC. Negative is over.

What "over" means here is not "overspent". A row is marked over when its projected final cost exceeds its budget — money already gone, money committed, and the estimate of what is still to come. An account can be flagged over on a day when it has spent almost nothing, because everything it will spend is already committed. That is the point: an overrun surfaces while there is still a week in which to act on it.

The ETC cell is the one place on the report you can overrule the arithmetic. Type a number and it is stored per account. Clear it and the default returns. A non-numeric entry is read as zero, which will quietly flatter the account, so type figures.

The TOTAL row is not a sum of the rows you can see. Totals are accumulated from the cents that were actually posted, and total EFC and total variance are derived from those sums. This is load-bearing: three rows of $100.50, rounded per row and then added, come to $303; summed as posted, they come to $301.50. The report agrees with the second number.

Money is carried in cents

Every figure in this layer is an integer number of cents (js/lib-money-math.js). Rounding is half away from zero, applied once, at the point a fraction of a cent is created — a multiplication or a percentage — and never on a sum. JavaScript's own Math.round is half-up, so it turns −2.5 into −2 and biases every negative variance toward zero; that is why it is not used.

The rule at the edge is the one that matters most to you: a money figure leaving the platform is written as a string with exactly two decimal places. A raw float is never put in a cell. Fifteen units of $1,061.64 is $15,924.60, but the float multiplication evaluates to 15924.600000000002; two budget lines of $15,920.30 and $4.30 evaluate to 15924.599999999999. Either one, written straight into a CSV, is what a producer's spreadsheet then adds up — and a column of those does not foot. The CSV writes 15924.60. Negative zero is written as 0.00, not -0.00, because a credit that is not there reads as a credit.

The CSV also defends the machine that opens it: any cell whose text begins =, +, -, @, a tab or a carriage return is prefixed with an apostrophe, so a vendor name typed on this site is read by Excel and Sheets as literal text rather than as a formula to run.

The screen rounds; the export does not. Cost report figures are displayed to the whole dollar. The CSV carries the cents. When the two appear to disagree by a few dollars across a column, the CSV is the one that is exact.

Snapshots, and who reads this report

Take weekly snapshot freezes the current totals into the record as week n, dated. Snapshots are written once and never edited afterwards.

The snapshot record is trimmed. Once more than sixty weeks are held, the list is cut back to the most recent fifty-two and the earliest weeks are discarded permanently. On a long production, export before you pass a year.

Three things read this report without being asked. The Producer Suite's top sheet shows the Money Room's EFC and its over/under figure beneath the grand total once any PO exists. The investor update letter pulls EFC from it and says so, and says so honestly when there is no cost report to pull from. And every account with both a budget and an actual is fed to the estimator on each render, so this production's real costs calibrate the next one's estimates. Nothing about that is reversible from here — it is another reason to keep an account number correct before it becomes history.
