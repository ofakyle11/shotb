Props & Wardrobe

@@CH13@@

Two departments, one habit of mind. Break the script down into things; put a number on each thing; find someone who has one; and keep a record of what it looked like on the day, so the next shot matches. Cinamate treats props and wardrobe as the same shape of problem, and the two modules are built to the same pattern deliberately.

On the accuracy of this chapter. Everything here was established by reading the files named in it — no part of it was observed in a running browser. The platform changes weekly. Where this page and the software disagree, the software is the authority and this page is out of date.

The props breakdown

The Props Department is props/index.html, over the engine props/lib-props.js (CProps). Its state lives in SB_Props_v1.

Rebuild from script reads the working screenplay out of SB_Timeline_v1.scriptText — under about forty characters it declines and tells you to load a screenplay in the Studio or the Writer first. It splits scenes through the one scene model, js/lib-scenes.js, then runs a fixed lexicon of 191 terms across nine categories over each scene body. A term matches on a word boundary, case-insensitively, with an optional plural.

Three consequences worth knowing before you trust the list. The lexicon is closed: a thing it does not name is not found, and goes in through + Add prop. Results are deduplicated by term, so one row covers every appearance of that word and quantity always starts at 1. And the match runs over the whole scene body, dialogue included — a gun somebody merely talks about is still listed. Scenes are recorded by their printed identity, so an A-scene stays an A-scene on the props list.

Rebuild replaces, it does not merge. Only rows you created with + Add prop survive a rebuild. Every automatically found row is discarded and regenerated, taking its quoted price, its hero and period flags and any hand-entered dimensions with it. Rebuild after a script revision; do not rebuild to refresh a list you have already priced.

What a prop costs

Pricing is the industry ten-per-cent rule: a week's rental is about a tenth of what the thing costs to replace. Cinamate carries a replacement value per category, and the whole estimate stands on it.

CategoryReplacement valuePriced as
Weapons & armory$1,200weekly; adds a licensed armorer
Picture vehicles$20,000$400 per shoot day
Furniture$900weekly
Electronics & tech$450weekly
Set dressing$180weekly
Hand props$75weekly
Greens & florals$1,200weekly
Food & consumables$60bought, $60 per shoot day
Specialty / oversize$3,500weekly

A hero prop triples that value, because it is built or dressed to close-up specification. Period multiplies it by 1.6, because period-correct stock is scarcer. The first rental week is charged in full and every week after it at 75%. Buying wins when renting passes 60% of the purchase price, which assumes roughly 40% asset recovery at wrap.

That break-even has a consequence you can rely on: because both sides of the comparison scale with the same value, every category priced by the ten per cent rule flips from rent to buy at eight rental weeks — hero and period props included. Vehicles and consumables never reach it, since they are priced by the day. Vehicles bill for twice their scene count in days, capped at the shoot length.

Weapons add an armorer at $650 a day, once per production rather than once per weapon. The engine will take a weapon-day count, but the page does not supply one, so the armorer line is three days on any schedule of three days or more. Ten per cent contingency is added on top of everything.

A gap, stated plainly. The engine honours a per-item replacement value, but the breakdown table offers no field for it — the editable columns are name, category, quantity, plan, hero, period, quoted price and size. Reading the source, there is no route in this page to set the value of one prop; the category default governs it. Use the quoted-price column for a real number.

Each row also carries a real-world size in feet, defaulted per category and overridable per item. The size button draws the prop beside a six-foot figure and answers one question outright: whether it passes a standard 3 ft × 6 ft 9 in doorway. → Set writes the prop into the active plan in SB_SetDesign_v1 at its true size; placing it twice moves it rather than littering the stage. A set plan has to exist first.

Sourcing, and the quote request

The directory holds twenty production hubs; twelve named houses are listed across six of them, and the remaining fourteen hubs are recognised but carry no houses yet. No directory entry carries a telephone number. Numbers arrive only from live map data, from the research service on your generation machine, or from your own typing — and numbers you type are saved against that production.

Use Advisor location reads the jurisdiction you modelled for tax incentives from SB_Budget_v1 and maps it to the nearest hub. A lookup then geocodes the city through OpenStreetMap's Nominatim and searches Overpass within 40 km, merging what it finds onto the directory: a map listing that matches a directory name fills in its phone and website rather than duplicating the card. Results are cached for a week in CIN_Learn_v1. Confirm every detail on the phone before you quote from it.

Section 3 assembles a quote request naming the production, the dates, the shooting area and every prop with its quantity, category and rent-or-buy intention, and asks for weekly rates, purchase where applicable, damage waiver and delivery. Copy gives you the whole thing. The email button truncates at 1,800 characters of encoded text, so send a long list by copy and paste.

Wardrobe: characters, looks and pieces

Wardrobe is wardrobe/index.html over wardrobe/lib-ward.js (CWard), stored in SB_Wardrobe_v1. Seeding reads the screenplay for dialogue cues — a short all-capitals line, suffixes such as (V.O.) and (CONT'D) folded together, followed by an actual line of dialogue — which is the same walk the casting office makes.

A look belongs to a character, names itself, lists the scenes it is worn in, and holds sizes, notes and pieces. Each piece has an item, a source — buy, rent, build or cast-own — an estimated cost and, later, an actual. The actual sits beside the estimate and never on top of it: overwriting an estimate with an invoice destroys the only record of what the department thought, and with it any way to find out whether it was right.

Two things that remove data. Deleting a look also deletes its continuity photographs from this browser, with no confirmation. And a character chip seeds a look from the legacy numeric scene list, which cannot tell 4A from 4 — after seeding, retype the scene box, which does accept A-scenes and ranges (1, 4A, 6-9).

What the department learns from an invoice

This is the part of both modules worth understanding properly. The department types an estimate; weeks later an invoice arrives for a different number; and until this loop existed the second number had nowhere to go, so next year's estimate was the same guess again. The reasoning is written out at wardrobe/lib-ward.js lines 169–206, and it copies recordQuote in props/lib-props.js deliberately. Three rules separate calibration from self-flattery.

It learns from the raw estimate, never the calibrated one. Feeding the corrected figure back in would teach the multiplier from its own output and compound it — the number would drift and always agree with itself. What goes to the learning layer is what you typed.

It reports the evidence it is actually applying. Every priced piece carries learnedN: how many past invoices stand behind the multiplier in front of you. A correction resting on two invoices is never shown as though it rested on fifty. The rollup prints the multiplier and its count together, per source.

Below the threshold it says so. Two invoices is the minimum. Under it the multiplier is exactly 1 and the page reads not calibrated · 1 of 2 invoice(s) needed — estimates for this source are exactly what you typed, rather than dressing a guess in arithmetic. Note the phrasing: the evidence that exists is still reported, even while it is not being used.

The account is chosen so that the learning means something. Wardrobe learns per source: a built garment overruns for different reasons than a rental does, and averaging them would teach nothing about either. Props learns per category, under props:handprop and its siblings. Both write into CIN_Learn_v1, which sits outside the per-project SB_ namespace on purpose, so what one production teaches survives into the next.

Nothing is learned until the film is wrapped. The learning layer refuses every observation unless the active project is marked wrapped in Projects. Mid-shoot, an actual is whatever has been invoiced so far against a whole-picture estimate — a department that has spent 12% in week two looks like an 88% underrun, and folding that in would drag every future estimate down. So typing an invoice during production is recorded on the piece and teaches nothing, and no confirmation appears. Mark the production wrapped when it is finished. A line also teaches only once: re-typing a corrected invoice against the same item does not re-teach it.

Where evidence does exist, the multiplier is a ratio of actual to estimate, weighted towards recent films and clamped so no single catastrophe can double or halve an account. Treat the size of a correction as a measure of how much correcting has been necessary — not as a measure of accuracy. The platform keeps a separate walk-forward record for that question, and it can come back negative. An operator who holds these three rules in mind will believe the number when it has been earned and disbelieve it when it has not, which is worth more than any feature.

Story days, and where they are uncertain

A story day is a day in the life of the story, not a day of the shoot. Scene 4A and scene 22 may be the same story day, in which case the shirt, the cut lip and the hair must match between them — even though one shoots in week one and the other in week three. No scene record holds this: a slugline says INT or EXT and a time of day, and nothing else states which day of the story it is. js/lib-storyday.js derives it, and the wardrobe page presents it as section 2.

The derivation compares each scene against the last printed time of day, so a run of CONTINUOUS headings does not lose the clock. Every boundary carries a confidence, and the honest half is the second column.

Certain — the script said soUncertain — the derivation had to choose
CONTINUOUS, SAME TIME, LATER, MOMENTS LATERDAY following DAY: nothing says whether a night passed
NEXT DAY, THREE DAYS LATER, THE DAY AFTERNIGHT following NIGHT: nothing says it is the same night
An explicit stamp on the heading — DAY 4DAY into NIGHT: read as the evening of the same day
NIGHT giving way to DAY — the sun came upA heading with no time of day printed at all
The first scene of the script opens day 1Flashback, dream, fantasy: a new day, but which one is unsaid

Uncertain scenes default to the same story day, which is the conservative reading: it makes the department match more often, and an unnecessary match costs a photograph while a missed one costs a reshoot. Every uncertain boundary is listed rather than hidden, and the page states the tally — so many boundaries the script settles, so many it does not. Walk that list with the script supervisor and set them by hand. A hand-set day always wins, is marked as set by hand, and still shows what it overruled.

A wrong story day is a continuity error on screen. Treat the derivation as a first pass. SB_StoryDays_v1 stores only your decisions — the overrides and any day names — and the derivation is recomputed from the script every time, so a rewrite never leaves stale days pretending to be current. Clear all overrides discards every hand-set day at once and cannot be undone.

The costume plot, changes and doubles

Section 3 is character against scene, grouped by story day and cross-referenced to the shooting order taken from the stripboard in SB_ScheduleBoard_v1. The board identifies a strip by its heading and its ordinal, never by the printed scene number, so the join is made here — and a strip that matches no scene in the script is reported rather than quietly dropped.

Changes are numbered per character in story order, which is what a supervisor calls out: MAGGIE #1, #2, #3. A look that comes back keeps its own number, because "back into change 2" is the instruction. Each change is then flagged: must match when it is worn in more than one scene, with a note when it spans several shooting days; shot out of story order, the classic trap where a garment's torn and bloodied state is arrived at on story day 2 and photographed on shooting day 9; and uncertain where the story day itself is not settled. Quick changes are measured inside the story day, where the clock actually runs, and the flag says whether both scenes shoot on the same day — a change on the floor with a dresser standing by, or a change on the truck. Two looks down for one scene is a conflict; a look pinned to a scene the screenplay does not contain is named as such.

Doubles come from the script. Six hazards are read from each scene — blood, rain, mud, tearing, fights and water. A look that meets one gets three multiples (one to wear, one to wreck, one spare), plus one for each further hazard scene, capped at six. The advice says outright that it is an estimate: confirm the count with stunts and SFX.

Continuity photographs

Photographs attach to a look. Each is scaled so its longest edge is at most 1,024 pixels, stored as JPEG, and stamped with the date and the active project. The bytes go into IndexedDB — database cinamate_wardrobe, store photos.

The vault does not carry your photographs. The project vault snapshots localStorage only. The photo references travel with the project and into every archive; the bytes stay in this browser. Switching projects therefore strands them, and an archive restored on a second machine shows empty frames. The scan on the page counts exactly this — yours, another production's, orphans no project points at any more, and references whose bytes are not on this device. Export photo pack is the only way to move them; keep the file. Purge orphans deletes bytes permanently after a confirmation.

Finally, Commit total to Money Room raises a purchase order against account 10000 for the wardrobe total, and the description records which figure it is — calibrated on past invoices, or an uncalibrated estimate.

All three engines are covered by node suites that run without a browser: scripts/test_props.mjs, scripts/test_wardrobe.mjs and scripts/test_storyday.mjs. Their fixtures are deliberately awkward — a FADE IN: preamble, printed scene numbers, an A-scene carrying a character's only second look, an I/E slugline and a shooting order that is not the story order — because those are the scripts a department actually receives.
