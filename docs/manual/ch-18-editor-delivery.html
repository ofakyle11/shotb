The Editor II — Assist Tools & Delivery

@@CH18@@

A cut that only exists in a browser tab is not a cut. This chapter covers the four assisted-editing tools, the frame sizes the Editor will render into, the encode that produces a viewable file, and the two interchange formats that hand the sequence to somebody else's finishing suite. Chapter 17 covered the cutting; this one covers getting out of the building.

How this chapter was made. Every statement below was established by reading the Editor's source. Nothing here was verified by operating the product, because the product is still being built. If the page in front of you contradicts this page, the page in front of you is right.

The assist suite

Three assist buttons sit under Assistant in the media bin; the fourth lives in the Inspector, on a selected clip. All four are deterministic signal processing running in your browser — arithmetic over an amplitude envelope or a luma histogram. Nothing is sent anywhere, no model is consulted, and the same input gives the same output every time.

Rough cut

Takes every picture row in the bin longer than 0.4 s whose media is present, in bin order, and lays them end to end on V1. Each clip is given a handle of 0.25 s off the head and the tail, or a quarter of the source's length if that is shorter. Scene numbers are read out of the bin row's name, not from any Studio metadata: the pattern is sc or scene followed by digits, case-insensitive, so SC01 Rooftop and Scene 4 wide both parse and Rooftop_take3 does not. Where the scene number changes between two consecutive clips the incoming clip gets a 0.75 s crossfade; everywhere else, a straight cut.

Tighten cut

Decodes each distinct source once, reduces channel 0 to an amplitude envelope at fifty samples per second, and looks for runs quieter than 0.04 lasting at least 0.35 s. Where such a run touches the head or tail of a clip's existing in/out points the trim is pulled past it, leaving a 0.12 s breath. Two limits matter: it only ever touches the ends of a clip, so silence in the middle of a take is found but never removed — that would be a cut the Editor did not make — and no clip is tightened below 0.4 s. The toast reports the seconds recovered, to a tenth.

Cut to beats

Requires a music block on A1; the first audio block is the one analysed. The same fifty-per-second envelope is scanned for rising peaks that exceed 1.5× the trailing one-second average, are above an absolute floor of 0.08, and are at least 0.25 s apart. Fewer than three beats and the tool declines. Otherwise the video track is re-sliced so that one clip occupies each beat interval, cycling through the clips already on the timeline; intervals shorter than 0.2 s are skipped, and each take is the shorter of the interval and the source's remaining length.

Destructive. Both Rough cut and Cut to beats discard the entire V1 track and rebuild it. Hand trims, splits, transitions and per-clip colour on the old clips are gone. Titles and audio are untouched. Both take an undo snapshot first, so Ctrl/Cmd+Z restores the previous track — but undo is held in memory only and does not survive a reload.

Auto colour

Select a clip and press Auto in the Inspector's Colour group. The current preview frame is drawn to a 160×90 scratch canvas, converted to luminance with the Rec. 709 weights and binned into a 256-bin histogram. Contrast is set from a 5th-to-95th-percentile stretch, clamped to the range 1 to 1.6; exposure pulls the mean toward mid-grey (118), clamped to 0.7 to 1.5. Saturation and warmth are left where they are — this balances, it does not grade. Two failure modes are worth knowing: if no frame is loaded the tool asks you to scrub onto the clip first, and a source that refuses a pixel read (a cross-origin Studio render, typically) reports that it blocks pixel reads and suggests re-importing it as a local file.

Frame size and the social presets

The Picture menu offers 720p (1280×720), 1080p (1920×1080), 480p (854×480), Social 9:16 (1080×1920) and Social 1:1 (1080×1080). The fitting rule is decided by shape, not by preset name: where the target is at least as tall as it is wide, the source is scaled to fill and the overflow is cropped symmetrically; where it is wider than tall, the source is scaled to fit and letterboxed. So both social presets crop, and both landscape presets pad.

The crop is not previewed. The preview canvas is fixed at 1280×720 in editor/index.html and nothing in editor/cut-ui.js resizes it when the Picture menu changes. Choosing Social 9:16 therefore alters the exported file and not the picture you are watching. Anything you have composed near the left or right edge of the 16:9 frame — a title's descender, a face, a sign — will be outside a 9:16 export and you will first see that in the finished MP4.

The export path

Pressing Export MP4 writes the Picture and rate selections onto the project, then renders the cut one frame at a time. For each frame index the source video element is seeked to the exact source time, the frame is drawn into an offscreen canvas at the target size through the clip's colour filter, titles are drawn over it, and the result is handed to the browser's VideoEncoder. Three H.264 configurations are tried in order — High 4.0, Main 3.1, Baseline 3.1 — and the first the browser reports as supported is used. Bitrate is width × height × rate × 0.12, so 1080p at 24 fps asks for roughly 6 Mbit/s. A keyframe is forced every two seconds.

Audio is mixed first and separately. Every video clip's own sound and every A1 block are rendered into one offline stereo mix at 48 kHz — gains applied, speed changes applied as playback rate — and encoded as AAC-LC at 128 kbit/s. A browser with no AudioEncoder, or one that refuses that configuration, gets a silent export and a toast saying video only.

The container is written by Cinamate's own MP4 writer, editor/lib-mp4.js: ftyp declaring isom/iso2/avc1/mp41, then moov, then one mdat. The video sample entry carries a colr box in nclx form declaring Rec. 709 primaries, transfer and matrix at limited range, so QuickTime, Resolve and Chrome do not each guess differently. The audio track carries an edit list skipping AAC's 1024 primed samples and trimming the tail the encoder rounded up, so the mix presents exactly as long as it was rendered.

What is and is not re-encoded

Everything is. There is no smart render, no stream copy, no passthrough: even a clip you neither trimmed nor graded is decoded, drawn to a canvas at the export size and compressed again. Every export is therefore a generation of loss, and each one starts from the file in the bin rather than from the last export, so exporting twice at two sizes gives two independent re-encodes rather than a master and a copy. The interchange files are the opposite case — an EDL or an OTIO re-encodes nothing, because neither contains picture. The MP4 is for viewing; turn over and finish elsewhere.

The compatibility export

A browser without VideoEncoder, or with no usable H.264 configuration, falls back to recording the preview in real time. That path produces a silent .webm, takes as long as the cut runs, and — because it captures the preview canvas — ignores the Picture menu and comes out at 1280×720.

Crossfades in an export. The outgoing frame a crossfade mixes against is cached only by the playback loop, from the preview canvas, at the moment the playhead crosses into a new clip. The export loop never refreshes it, so on the reading of the source a crossfade in an export mixes against an empty cache — effectively a hard cut — or, after playback, against a stale still. Fades to and from black are computed from the timeline alone and do render correctly. Treat crossfades as a preview convenience and place them in the finishing suite, where the interchange formats do not carry them either.

Turnover: the EDL

The Editor's EDL button writes a CMX-style list named from the cut. It opens with TITLE:, then FCM: NON-DROP FRAME, then a comment carrying the frame rate. Each clip becomes one event, numbered from 001, on reel AX, with source in/out and record in/out; a * FROM CLIP NAME: comment follows, and a * SPEED: comment where the clip is not at 1×.

Line breaks in labels are folded, deliberately. Every field written into an EDL passes through edlField in js/safe-url.js, which replaces newlines, carriage returns and U+2028/U+2029 with a single space, removes control characters, and truncates at 200 characters. An EDL is line-oriented: a newline inside a clip label does not make a long label, it ends the comment and begins a record, and a newline inside the cut's name emits a second FCM: line above the real one — CMX readers take the first, and the whole reel then conforms at the wrong timecode base. So a label whose line breaks reappear as spaces in the export, or a very long name that stops at 200 characters, is the guard working. Your project is not corrupted, and the text either side of a fold is preserved.

The stamped rate is an integer

The rate written into the file is round(fps). A 29.97 master stamps * FRAME RATE: 30 and a 23.976 master stamps 24. That is the convention, not a rounding error: non-drop timecode counts whole frames, and the drop/non-drop distinction is what the FCM: line carries. What matters — and what the test suite pins — is that a 29.97 master is never silently stamped 24. An unreadable rate does fall back to 24, visibly, in one place.

The Studio's own EDL

The Studio at /timeline/ exports a second, differently shaped EDL through timeline/timeline-export.js. Its title is fixed, its reels are named CLIP_01 upward, and each event carries * LABEL:, * SOURCE FILE:, * DESC: and, where set, * TRANSITION: comments — all through the same edlField guard. It exports approved clips that have video if there are any and otherwise every clip, and floors a zero-length clip to one frame, because an event whose record in equals its record out is one no conform will accept.

Its rate comes from the Master frame rate picker (tlFps), offering 23.976, 24, 25 (PAL), 29.97 and 30. masterFps() in timeline/timeline.js reads that picker at the moment of export, writes it onto the Studio state, and passes the result to both export calls — the EDL and the project JSON, whose fps is written explicitly because a machine reads it back. This is a separate setting from the Editor's own rate menu, which offers 24 and 30; set both.

Turnover: OTIO

The OTIO for Resolve button writes OpenTimelineIO JSON — Timeline.1 containing a Stack.1, a Track.1 named V1, and one Clip.2 per video clip with a TimeRange.1 source range. A speed change becomes a LinearTimeWarp.1 effect. Audio blocks are laid onto lanes named A1, A2 and upward: each lane keeps a cursor in frames, the gap before a cue is the distance from that cursor rather than the cue's absolute start, and a cue that would overlap the lane's cursor opens a new lane rather than being shoved late. Every time value is rounded once, into frames, at the project rate.

Media references are the thing to check. A row that came from the Studio carries its render's address; a row imported from disk carries only the file name, with no path. Either way expect to relink, and keep the originals — an object URL from a previous session died with the tab. The EDL carries no source path at all, so an EDL alone can never relink; prefer the OTIO where the reader supports it.

What the Editor does not deliver

No DCP. No IMF. No conform to a camera master — there is no relink step and no concept of a camera original anywhere in the Editor; what it renders is what is in the bin. No log or wide-gamut pipeline, no LUT, no high dynamic range: the export is an 8-bit H.264 encode off a canvas, tagged Rec. 709 at limited range, and that tag is a truthful description of the pixels rather than an aspiration. No 5.1 printmaster, no M&E split, no QC report — the mix is one stereo pair. The Post module's own deliverables list (DCP, 5.1 printmaster, M&E, ProRes master, QC report) is a list of things that happen somewhere else; the Editor's contribution to it is the turnover.

The delivery checklist

  1. Set the frame rate before you trim, not after. Check the Editor's rate menu and, if you are exporting from the Studio as well, the Master frame rate picker — they are separate settings.
  2. Open the bin and confirm nothing is marked media missing. A missing source keeps its row and its place in the sequence, so the timeline looks complete when it is not.
  3. Play the cut through once at 1×. It is the only thing that populates the crossfade cache, and it is when a failed audio decode shows itself as a blank waveform strip.
  4. Choose the Picture size last, remembering that a social preset crops a frame you have been watching uncropped.
  5. Export the MP4 for viewing, and read the toast — it gives the file size and whether audio made it in.
  6. Export both interchange files if anyone downstream will touch this cut: the EDL for a legacy conform, the OTIO for Resolve or Premiere.
  7. Open the EDL in a text editor before you send it. Confirm one FCM: line, an event count that matches your clip count, and a frame rate you recognise as the integer form of your master.
  8. Send the media with the turnover. Neither file contains picture, and the OTIO's references will not resolve on another machine.
  9. Back the production up. The cut lives in local storage under SB_Cut_v1 and imported media in IndexedDB; Projects → Export backup is what makes both travel.

A successful export is recorded alongside the cut — timestamp, duration, frame size, rate and whether audio made it in. That record is not shown in the Editor; it is read elsewhere, which is why a production reads as mastered on the dashboard only once something has actually been exported.
